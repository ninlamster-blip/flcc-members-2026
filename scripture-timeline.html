Creation In the Beginning
Creation
Genesis 1–2

God speaks existence into being — light, sky, land, seas, living creatures — and crowns creation with humanity made in His image. The world is declared very good. This is not merely origin; it is the foundation of all meaning, dignity, and relationship.

Key Figures

The Fall
Genesis 3

Adam and Eve disobey God, sin enters creation, and the relationship between God and humanity is fractured. Yet in judgment, God speaks the first promise of redemption — the seed of the woman will crush the serpent. This protoevangelium is the lens through which all of Scripture must be read.

Key Figures
Adam Eve The Serpent

Noah and the Flood
Genesis 6–9

Humanity's corruption reaches its fullness, and God sends judgment through a great flood. Noah, a righteous man, is preserved by faith. After the waters recede, God establishes a covenant with all creation, sealed by a rainbow — God's judgment is real, and so is His commitment to preserve His world.

Key Figures
Noah Shem Ham Japheth
Genesis 9:13 Hebrews 11:7
Tower of Babel
Genesis 11

Human pride reaches its zenith as the nations attempt to build a tower to heaven. God confuses their language and scatters them across the earth. This sets the stage for God's call of Abraham — the answer to the problem of scattered, divided humanity is not human achievement but divine election and promise.

Patriarchs c. 2100 – 1700 BC
The Call of Abraham
Genesis 12; 15; 17

God calls Abram out of Ur of the Chaldeans with a staggering promise: a land, a great nation, and blessing to all the families of the earth. This Abrahamic Covenant is the spine of redemptive history. Every subsequent act of God flows from this unconditional promise — ultimately fulfilled in Jesus Christ, the true seed of Abraham.

Abraham and Isaac — The Binding
Genesis 22

God tests Abraham by commanding him to sacrifice his son Isaac. Abraham obeys in faith, and at the last moment God provides a ram. Mount Moriah — the site of this substitutionary provision — is later identified as Jerusalem, the very place where God's own Son would become the ultimate sacrifice. The substitution foreshadowed points forward unmistakably.

Isaac, Jacob, and the Twelve Tribes
Genesis 25–35

The promise passes to Isaac, then to Jacob — whom God renames Israel. Through his twelve sons, the twelve tribes of Israel are formed. God's covenant purpose is not thwarted by human weakness; Jacob's deceptions, family dysfunction, and flight from home all become instruments of divine sovereignty. The nation that will receive the Law and inherit the land begins here.

Key Figures
Isaac Rebekah Jacob Esau Rachel Leah
Genesis 28:15 Romans 9:13
Joseph — Betrayal, Sovereignty, and Egypt
Genesis 37–50

Joseph, Jacob's favored son, is sold into slavery by his brothers, falsely accused, and imprisoned. Yet through a series of divine providences, he rises to rule Egypt and saves the ancient world from famine — including his own family. Joseph's story is a masterclass in divine sovereignty through human suffering, and a profound foreshadowing of the one who would be betrayed, suffer, and then save.

Exodus c. 1446 BC
Moses and the Burning Bush
Exodus 3–4

After 400 years of slavery in Egypt, God appears to Moses in a burning bush and reveals His name: I AM WHO I AM. This self-disclosure of God's eternal, self-existent nature is foundational to all theology. God commissions Moses to deliver Israel — a man who protests his own inadequacy becomes the instrument of the greatest deliverance in the Old Testament.

Key Figures
Moses Aaron Jethro
Exodus 3:14 John 8:58
The Ten Plagues and the Passover
Exodus 7–12

Through ten escalating plagues, God dismantles Egypt's power and exposes the impotence of its gods. The tenth and final plague — the death of the firstborn — is averted for Israel by the blood of a lamb on their doorposts. The Passover is the defining saving event of the Old Testament, and its fulfillment is found in Jesus Christ, our Passover Lamb, who was sacrificed for us.

Crossing the Red Sea
Exodus 14

Trapped between the Egyptian army and the sea, Israel witnesses the most dramatic deliverance yet — the Red Sea parts, Israel walks through on dry ground, and Egypt's army is destroyed. Paul identifies this crossing as a kind of baptism — the old life left behind, new life entered through water. The pattern of judgment and salvation is set for all Scripture.

Exodus 14:31 1 Corinthians 10:2
Mount Sinai and the Ten Commandments
Exodus 19–20

At Mount Sinai, God enters into a covenant with Israel and gives the Law — not as a means of earning salvation, but as a response to it. God first declares "I am the Lord your God who brought you out of Egypt" before giving any commands. The Law reveals God's character and humanity's need for a mediator — ultimately fulfilled in Christ, who did not abolish the Law but completed it.

Key Events
Ten Commandments Mosaic Covenant God's Holiness

The Tabernacle
Exodus 25–40

God instructs Moses to build the Tabernacle — a portable sanctuary where God's glory would dwell among His people. Every element points forward: the altar of sacrifice, the veil, the ark of the covenant, the high priest. The writer of Hebrews explains that the entire Tabernacle system is a shadow of the heavenly reality fulfilled in Christ, our great High Priest.

Exodus 25:8 Hebrews 9:11
Conquest & Judges c. 1406 – 1050 BC
Joshua and the Conquest of Canaan
Joshua 1–12

After forty years of wilderness wandering, a new generation enters the Promised Land under Joshua's leadership. The walls of Jericho fall at God's command. City by city, Israel takes possession of the land God had promised to Abraham centuries earlier. Joshua (whose name means "salvation") is a type of Jesus — the one who brings God's people into their inheritance.

Key Figures
Joshua Caleb Rahab
Joshua 1:9
The Period of the Judges
Judges 2–16

After Joshua's death, Israel enters a cyclical pattern: abandonment of God, oppression by enemies, crying out to God, and deliverance through a judge. Figures like Deborah, Gideon, and Samson appear — each delivering Israel from oppressors, yet each also deeply flawed. The book ends with the haunting summary: "Everyone did what was right in their own eyes." Israel desperately needs a king.

Key Judges
Deborah Gideon Jephthah Samson
Judges 2:16 Judges 21:25
United Kingdom c. 1050 – 931 BC
King Saul — The People's King
1 Samuel 8–15

Israel demands a king like the surrounding nations. God grants their request, warning them of the consequences. Saul is tall, handsome, and initially promising, but his repeated disobedience and failure to trust God result in his rejection. His life is a sobering study in the difference between outward religious performance and genuine heart obedience.

Key Figures
Saul Samuel Jonathan
1 Samuel 15:22
King David — A Man After God's Heart
1 Samuel 16 – 2 Samuel 24

David is anointed king as a shepherd boy and grows to be Israel's greatest king. He unites the nation, establishes Jerusalem as the capital, defeats all enemies, and writes many of the Psalms. The Davidic Covenant promises an eternal throne, fulfilled ultimately in Jesus — the Son of David. Even David's gravest sin and the Psalms of repentance that follow reveal a man whose heart fundamentally turns toward God.

Key Figures
David Nathan Bathsheba Solomon
2 Samuel 7:16 Acts 2:30
Solomon and the Temple
1 Kings 1–11

Solomon begins his reign with unparalleled wisdom and builds the Temple in Jerusalem — the permanent dwelling place of God's glory. The dedication ceremony is breathtaking. Yet Solomon's heart turns away through foreign wives and idolatry, and the kingdom is torn from his son. Wisdom without faithfulness is ultimately futile. The Temple points forward to the One who declared "destroy this temple, and I will raise it again in three days."

1 Kings 8:27 John 2:19
Divided Kingdom 931 – 586 BC
The Kingdom Divides — Israel and Judah
1 Kings 12 – 2 Kings 17

After Solomon's death, the kingdom splits into two: Israel (north, 10 tribes) and Judah (south, 2 tribes). The northern kingdom is immediately plagued by false worship and corrupt kings. God sends prophets — especially Elijah and Elisha — to call the people back. The northern kingdom is eventually destroyed by Assyria in 722 BC, the southern kingdom of Judah survives longer under the Davidic line.

Exile 605 – 538 BC
Babylon and the Exile
2 Kings 25; Jeremiah; Lamentations

After repeated warnings, Babylon under Nebuchadnezzar destroys Jerusalem, burns the Temple, and deports the population. This is the darkest moment in Israel's history. Yet in the midst of judgment, the prophets speak extraordinary hope: a new covenant, a new heart, the Spirit poured out, the valley of dry bones restored. God's purposes are not defeated — they are refined.

Key Prophets
Jeremiah Ezekiel Daniel Isaiah
Jeremiah 29:11 Jeremiah 31:33 Ezekiel 37:4
Daniel in Babylon
Daniel 1–12

Daniel and his three friends — Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah — maintain covenant faithfulness in the most hostile pagan environment. Through visions and miraculous deliverances (the fiery furnace, the lion's den), God demonstrates sovereignty over all kingdoms. Daniel's visions of the Ancient of Days, the Son of Man, and the seventy weeks provide the apocalyptic framework that Jesus and the apostles will draw upon.

Daniel 2:44 Daniel 7:13
Return from Exile 538 – 400 BC
Ezra, Nehemiah, and the Restoration
Ezra 1–10; Nehemiah 1–13

By decree of Cyrus the Great (as prophesied by Isaiah), the exiles return to Jerusalem in waves. Zerubbabel leads the rebuilding of the Temple, Ezra leads spiritual renewal around the Word, and Nehemiah oversees the reconstruction of the city walls. The return is a foretaste of the greater restoration and New Creation to come — but the people quickly see that they need more than rebuilt walls; they need transformed hearts.

Intertestamental Period 400 BC – 4 BC
Four Hundred Years of Silence
Malachi – Matthew

Between Malachi and Matthew, God sends no new prophet for approximately 400 years. The world is not silent — Alexander the Great spreads Greek culture, Rome rises to power, synagogues emerge throughout the Jewish world, and the Septuagint (Greek Old Testament) is translated, preparing the world for the gospel. Malachi ends with a promise: Elijah will come before the great day of the Lord. The world waits.

The Fullness of Time

The Life of Jesus Christ

The center of all history, the fulfillment of all Scripture

01
The Birth of Christ
Matthew 1–2 Luke 1–2

The eternal Son of God takes on human flesh in Bethlehem, born of a virgin. Angels announce His birth to shepherds; Magi travel from the East. God has entered His creation.

Fulfilled: Isaiah 7:14 · Micah 5:2 · Hosea 11:1
02
Baptism and Temptation
Matthew 3–4 Mark 1 Luke 3–4

Jesus is baptized by John and the Trinity is revealed — Father's voice, Son in water, Spirit as a dove. He then faces forty days of temptation in the wilderness, succeeding where Israel failed.

Fulfilled: Isaiah 42:1 · Deuteronomy 8:3
03
Calling the Disciples
Matthew 4 Mark 1–3 Luke 5–6

Jesus calls twelve ordinary men — fishermen, a tax collector, a zealot — to follow Him. He will form a new community, a new Israel, built on the foundation of these apostles.

Connection: Twelve tribes of Israel · Exodus 24
04
Miracles and Signs
Matthew 8–9 John 2–11

Jesus heals the sick, gives sight to the blind, raises the dead, stills storms, and feeds thousands. These signs announce that the Kingdom of God has arrived — creation is being restored. John selects seven signs as a new creation narrative.

Fulfilled: Isaiah 35:5-6 · Isaiah 61:1
05
The Sermon on the Mount
Matthew 5–7 Luke 6

Jesus gives the definitive interpretation of God's Law, deepening rather than abolishing it. He describes the character of Kingdom citizens — the Beatitudes — and teaches the Lord's Prayer. He is the new Moses on a new mountain.

Connection: Mount Sinai · Exodus 19–20
06
Parables of the Kingdom
Matthew 13 Mark 4 Luke 15

Jesus teaches the nature of God's Kingdom in parables — the Prodigal Son, the Good Samaritan, the Lost Sheep, the Hidden Treasure. These stories reveal both the scandal of grace and the costliness of discipleship.

Fulfilled: Psalm 78:2 · "I will open my mouth in parables"
07
The Transfiguration
Matthew 17 Mark 9 Luke 9

On a high mountain, Jesus' glory shines through His humanity. Moses and Elijah — representing the Law and the Prophets — appear with Him. The Father's voice confirms: "This is my Son, listen to him." All of Scripture points to this moment.

Connection: Moses, Elijah · Deuteronomy 18:15
08
The Triumphal Entry
Matthew 21 Mark 11 Luke 19 John 12

Jesus enters Jerusalem on a donkey to shouts of "Hosanna!" — consciously presenting Himself as Israel's King. The week that follows contains the most theologically dense events in human history.

Fulfilled: Zechariah 9:9 · Psalm 118:26
09
The Crucifixion
Matthew 27 Mark 15 Luke 23 John 19

Jesus is crucified between two criminals outside Jerusalem. The one who knew no sin becomes sin for us. The Temple veil tears in two. The darkness at noon, the earthquake, the centurion's confession — all of creation responds. The Passover Lamb has been slain.

Fulfilled: Isaiah 53 · Psalm 22 · Zechariah 12:10
10
The Resurrection
Matthew 28 Mark 16 Luke 24 John 20–21

On the third day, Jesus rises bodily from the dead. The tomb is empty. He appears to Mary Magdalene, to the disciples, to more than five hundred people. Death has been defeated. The new creation has begun. If Christ has not been raised, Paul writes, our faith is futile — but He has been raised.

Fulfilled: Psalm 16:10 · Jonah 1:17 · Isaiah 53:10
11
The Great Commission and Ascension
Matthew 28 Luke 24 John 20

Jesus commissions His disciples to make disciples of all nations. He ascends to the Father's right hand — not absent but reigning as King. His ascension is His enthronement. From there He pours out the Spirit and intercedes for His people.

Fulfilled: Daniel 7:13-14 · Psalm 110:1
Acts of the Apostles

The Early Church

The Spirit poured out, the gospel spreading to the ends of the earth.

Acts 2
Pentecost

Fifty days after the Resurrection, the Holy Spirit is poured out on 120 gathered disciples. They speak in the languages of the nations — a reversal of Babel. Peter preaches and 3,000 are baptized. The Church is born.

Acts 3–7
Peter and the Jerusalem Church

The early church devotes itself to teaching, fellowship, breaking of bread, and prayer. Signs and wonders accompany the apostles. Stephen, the first martyr, is stoned while seeing Jesus standing at God's right hand.

Acts 8–9
Persecution and Expansion

The scattering caused by persecution becomes the means of gospel expansion. Philip evangelizes Samaria. The Ethiopian eunuch is baptized on a desert road — the gospel is already moving toward Africa.

Acts 9
The Conversion of Paul

Saul of Tarsus — the church's most fierce persecutor — encounters the risen Christ on the road to Damascus. "Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?" The greatest enemy of the church becomes its greatest missionary.

Acts 10–11
The Gentile Pentecost

God gives Peter a vision that breaks down the Jew/Gentile barrier. The Roman centurion Cornelius and his household receive the Holy Spirit. The door to the nations is opened, and Peter reports: "God shows no favoritism."

Acts 13–28
Paul's Missionary Journeys

Paul makes three missionary journeys across the Mediterranean world, planting churches in Asia Minor, Greece, and beyond. He is beaten, imprisoned, shipwrecked — yet the gospel advances relentlessly. He arrives in Rome, the heart of the empire.

Romans – Jude
The Letters of the New Testament

The apostles write to the churches they have planted, teaching the implications of the gospel for every area of life. Justification, sanctification, the church, the resurrection, the return of Christ — all are addressed with theological depth and pastoral care.

Acts 28
The Gospel Reaches Rome

Acts ends with Paul under house arrest in Rome, but the final words are triumphant: "He proclaimed the kingdom of God and taught about the Lord Jesus Christ — with all boldness and without hindrance!" The story is not over.

The End and the New Beginning

The Book of Revelation

The Apocalypse of John — not a book of fear, but of hope. The Lamb who was slain reigns, and all things are made new.

Revelation 1
The Vision on Patmos

The apostle John, exiled on the island of Patmos, receives a vision of the risen Christ — eyes like blazing fire, feet like bronze, a voice like rushing waters. This Christ is not the suffering servant but the reigning King. John falls at His feet as though dead.

Revelation 2–3
Letters to the Seven Churches

Christ dictates letters to seven actual churches in Asia Minor — Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Thyatira, Sardis, Philadelphia, Laodicea. Each receives both commendation and correction. These letters address every church in every age: faithfulness, compromise, endurance, and returning to first love.

Revelation 4–5
The Throne Room of Heaven

John is caught up to the throne room of God — surrounded by four living creatures and twenty-four elders, the crystal sea, the seven spirits, and ceaseless worship. The Lamb, looking as if slain, takes the scroll. Heaven erupts: "Worthy is the Lamb who was slain!" All creation joins the chorus.

Revelation 6–19
The Unfolding of History

Through seals, trumpets, and bowls, John sees the full scope of history from the perspective of heaven — persecution, cosmic upheaval, the triumph of the beast, the judgment of Babylon. Yet woven throughout are visions of the redeemed — a great multitude from every nation, standing before the Lamb, clothed in white.

Revelation 19
The Return of Christ

Heaven opens and the King of kings appears on a white horse — the Word of God, with a robe dipped in blood. He comes not as servant but as conqueror, to judge and to make war in righteousness. Every knee will bow before this King.

Revelation 20
Final Judgment

Satan is bound, then released, then cast into the lake of fire. Death and Hades give up their dead. The books are opened. The Book of Life is the final word. Those whose names are written there are not condemned — they have already been judged in their representative, Jesus Christ.

Revelation 21–22
New Heaven, New Earth, New Jerusalem

The first things have passed away. God declares: "I am making everything new!" The New Jerusalem descends from heaven — the dwelling of God is now with humanity. No more death, mourning, crying, or pain. The river of life flows from the throne. The tree of life yields its fruit. Eden is restored, magnified, glorified. The Grand Story of Scripture ends where it began — with God dwelling with His people — but now in indestructible, eternal glory. Come, Lord Jesus.

"He who testifies to these things says, 'Yes, I am coming soon.' Amen. Come, Lord Jesus."
Revelation 22:20
